# DDD case study: Marketnovus

Appendix A, PDF pages 299-313. Five bounded contexts, the mistakes made in each,
and a discussion that restates the book's rules as consequences.

Two stretches are **pack-shared** and citable by any skill: pp.303-304, which
print the three by-the-book aggregate rules, and pp.309-313, the discussion.

## The company, pp.299-300

- Marketnovus allowed you to outsource all of your marketing-related chores: experts would come up with a marketing strategy, and copywriters and graphic designers would produce creative material [DDD app.A p.299] `RULE`
- All the leads generated by the advertising campaigns would be handled by Marketnovus's sales agents, who would make the calls and sell the product [DDD app.A p.300] `RULE`
- The analysis department was in charge of the optimization opportunities in the marketing process [DDD app.A p.300] `RULE`
- The first version of the system implemented contracts and integrations with external publishers, a catalog for creative materials, and a campaign management solution [DDD app.A p.300] `RULE`

## Bounded context 1 — Marketing, pp.301-302

- The architectural style of the first solution could be summarized as aggregates everywhere: each and every noun in the requirements was proclaimed as an aggregate [DDD app.A p.301] `RULE`
- Those were no aggregates: they didn't provide any transactional boundaries, and they had almost no behavior in them [DDD app.A p.301] `RULE`
- All the business logic was implemented in an enormous service layer [DDD app.A p.301] `RULE`
- When you aim to implement a domain model but end up with the active record pattern, it is often termed an anemic domain model antipattern [DDD app.A p.301] `RULE`
- Despite the flawed architecture, a robust ubiquitous language allowed the team to grasp the business domain in no time and deliver working software [DDD app.A p.301] `RULE`

## Bounded context 2 — CRM, pp.302-304 — pack-shared

- Awkward prefixes were being added to the aggregate names, and those prefixes were never used in conversations with the domain experts [DDD app.A p.302] `RULE`
- Bounded contexts solve exactly that issue: they protect the consistency of the ubiquitous language [DDD app.A p.303] `RULE`
- Aggregates were being treated as data structures, but they play a much larger role by protecting the consistency of the system's data [DDD app.A p.303] `RULE`

### The three by-the-book rules, p.303

The same three rules as chapter 6, restated as a checklist the team vowed to
follow. Any skill may cite them.

1. Each transaction would affect only one instance of an aggregate [DDD app.A p.303] `RULE`
2. Instead of an ORM, each aggregate itself would define the transactional scope [DDD app.A p.303] `RULE`
3. The service layer would go on a very strict diet, and all the business logic would be refactored into the corresponding aggregates [DDD app.A p.303] `RULE`

### What broke anyway, pp.303-304

- Implementing the business logic in stored procedures produced an implicit bounded context whose boundary dissected one of the most complex business entities [DDD app.A pp.303-304] `RULE`
- The result was two teams working on the same business component, with minimal interaction between them [DDD app.A p.304] `RULE`
- The models were inconsistent, knowledge was duplicated, and the same rules were implemented twice [DDD app.A p.304] `RULE`
- The only way out of this mess was to completely rewrite the aggregate, this time with proper boundaries [DDD app.A p.304] `RULE`
- The understanding that evolved: build a ubiquitous language, protect its integrity using bounded contexts, and implement a proper domain model everywhere [DDD app.A p.304] `RULE`

## Bounded context 3 — Event crunchers, pp.305-306

- An implicit subdomain was spread across two contexts: whenever the process had to be modified, changes had to be introduced in both [DDD app.A p.305] `RULE`
- Since the company didn't make money out of the way it moved data around, and there were no off-the-shelf solutions, it resembled a supporting subdomain, designed with layered architecture and simple transaction scripts [DDD app.A p.305] `RULE`
- Those simple flags evolved into a real business logic, with complex rules and invariants: what started out as transaction scripts evolved into a full-fledged core business subdomain [DDD app.A p.305] `RULE`
- Nothing good happens when you implement complex business logic as transaction scripts: the result was a very big ball of mud [DDD app.A p.305] `RULE`
- The logic was refactored into an event-sourced domain model, with other bounded contexts subscribing to its events [DDD app.A p.306] `RULE`

## Bounded context 4 — Bonuses, pp.306-307

- The subdomain was categorized as supporting: nothing new was being invented, no money was made out of the process, and an existing implementation would have been bought [DDD app.A p.306] `RULE`
- The solution was designed accordingly: active record objects, orchestrated by a smart service layer [DDD app.A p.306] `RULE`
- As the system evolved, it gradually mutated into a core subdomain: ways were found to make money out of these processes [DDD app.A p.307] `RULE`
- The striking difference from the event crunchers project was the ubiquitous language [DDD app.A p.307] `RULE`
- As the domain's complexity grew, the language used by the domain experts got more complicated as well, until it could no longer be modeled using active records [DDD app.A p.307] `RULE`
- That realization allowed noticing the need for a change in the design much earlier [DDD app.A p.307] `RULE`

## Bounded context 5 — The Marketing Hub, pp.308-309

- Management defined the business domain as a new profit opportunity, so it was treated as a core business domain: event-sourced domain model and CQRS [DDD app.A p.308] `RULE`
- The microservices were approached with the assumption that the smaller the service was, the better, so service boundaries were drawn around the aggregates [DDD app.A p.308] `RULE`
- In DDD lingo, each aggregate became a bounded context on its own [DDD app.A p.308] `RULE`
- As the system grew, those services became more and more chatty, and almost each service required data from all the other services [DDD app.A p.308] `RULE`
- What was intended to be a decoupled system ended up being a distributed monolith: an absolute nightmare to maintain [DDD app.A p.308] `RULE`

### The real problem, p.309 — pack-shared

- Behind that complex architecture stood a very simple business logic, one so simple that it could have been implemented using plain active records [DDD app.A p.309] `RULE`
- The businesspeople were looking to profit by leveraging existing relationships with other companies, and not through the use of clever algorithms [DDD app.A p.309] `RULE`
- The technical complexity ended up being much higher than the business complexity [DDD app.A p.309] `RULE`
- To describe such discrepancies in complexities we use the term accidental complexity, and the initial design ended up being exactly that [DDD app.A p.309] `RULE`

This page is why business complexity and technical complexity are separate
inputs in `../rules/ddd-required-inputs.md`. Neither can be read off a
repository.

## The discussion, pp.309-313 — pack-shared

### Ubiquitous language, pp.309-310

- The presence of a ubiquitous language has been a major predictor of a project's success [DDD app.A p.309] `RULE`
- A robust ubiquitous language compensated for the architectural shortcomings and allowed the project's goals to be delivered [DDD app.A p.309] `RULE`
- Ubiquitous language is not optional, regardless of whether you're working on a core, supporting, or generic subdomain [DDD app.A p.310] `RULE`
- Invest in the ubiquitous language as early as possible [DDD app.A p.310] `RULE`
- It requires immense effort and patience to fix a language if it has been spoken for a while in a company [DDD app.A p.310] `RULE`
- The implementation was fixed, but for years some people were still using the conflicting terms defined in the initial implementation [DDD app.A p.310] `RULE`

### Subdomains, pp.310-312

- It can be challenging to identify a subdomain's type [DDD app.A p.310] `RULE`
- Identify the subdomains at the granularity level that is relevant to the software system you are building [DDD app.A p.310] `RULE`
- It's not enough to identify a subdomain's type: you also have to be aware of the possible evolutions of the subdomain into another type [DDD app.A p.310] `RULE`
- Failing to properly identify a subdomain can be a costly mistake [DDD app.A p.311] `RULE`

The reverse mapping, which turns a business question into a technical one:

1. Choose the business logic implementation pattern that fits the requirements at hand, with no speculation or gold plating [DDD app.A p.311] `RULE`
2. Map the chosen pattern to a suitable subdomain type [DDD app.A p.311] `RULE`
3. Verify the identified subdomain type with the business vision [DDD app.A p.311] `RULE`

The two branches p.311 prints for a mismatch, and refuses to resolve alone:

| Mismatch | What the page says | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| The business thinks something is core, but you can hack it in a day | A sign that you need to look for finer-grained subdomains, or that questions should be raised about the viability of that business | [DDD app.A p.311] `HEURISTIC` |
| The business takes a subdomain to be supporting, but it can only be implemented with a domain model or an event-sourced domain model | Either the requirements ended up with accidental business complexity and can be simplified, or the business does not yet realize it employs this subdomain to gain a competitive edge | [DDD app.A pp.311-312] `RULE` |
| anything else | — | HALT (`AMBIGUOUS_IN_BOOK`) |

- Reversing the relationship between subdomains and tactical design decisions creates an additional dialogue between you and the business [DDD app.A p.311] `RULE`
- Never ignore pain when implementing the system's business logic: it is a crucial signal to evolve and improve either the model or the tactical design decisions [DDD app.A p.312] `RULE`
- If the type has changed, talk with the domain experts to understand the business context [DDD app.A p.312] `RULE`

### Boundaries of bounded contexts, pp.312-313

The four strategies tried, with the one that is forbidden outright:

| Strategy | What happened | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Linguistic boundaries | The initial monolith was split into two contexts to protect their ubiquitous languages | [DDD app.A p.312] `RULE` |
| Subdomain-based boundaries | Many subdomains were implemented in their own bounded contexts | [DDD app.A p.312] `RULE` |
| Entity-based boundaries | Limited success in one project, and it worked in others | [DDD app.A p.312] `RULE` |
| Suicidal boundaries | An aggregate was dissected into two different bounded contexts | [DDD app.A p.312] `RULE` |
| anything else | — | HALT (`AMBIGUOUS_IN_BOOK`) |

The book's words for the fourth row are the shortest rule in the appendix:

```ddd-quote [DDD app.A p.312]
Never try this at home, okay?
```

And its words for choosing among the first three are a refusal:

```ddd-quote [DDD app.A p.312]
Which of these strategies is the recommended one? None of them fits in all cases.
```

- It was much safer to extract a service out of a bigger one than to start with services that are too small [DDD app.A p.312] `RULE`
- Hence, we preferred to start with bigger boundaries and decompose them later, as more knowledge was acquired about the business [DDD app.A p.312] `PREFERENCE`
- The less you know about the business domain, the wider the initial boundaries; this heuristic served us well [DDD app.A p.312] `HEURISTIC`
- Throughout the evolution of the bounded contexts, we stayed in the range of the safe boundaries [DDD app.A pp.312-313] `RULE`
- Going past the safe boundaries was avoided by doing the refactoring only after gaining enough knowledge of the business domain [DDD app.A p.313] `HEURISTIC`

### The final sequence, p.313

1. Always start by building a ubiquitous language with the domain experts, to learn as much as possible about the business domain [DDD app.A p.313] `RULE`
2. In the case of conflicting models, decompose the solution into bounded contexts, following the linguistic boundaries of the ubiquitous language [DDD app.A p.313] `RULE`
3. Identify the subdomains' boundaries and their types in each bounded context [DDD app.A p.313] `RULE`
4. For each subdomain, choose an implementation strategy by using tactical design heuristics [DDD app.A p.313] `HEURISTIC`
5. Verify the initial subdomain types with those resulting from the tactical design, and in cases of mismatching types, discuss them with the business [DDD app.A p.313] `RULE`
6. As more domain knowledge is acquired, decompose the bounded contexts further into contexts with narrower boundaries [DDD app.A p.313] `RULE`

- The main difference from the starting vision is that we went from aggregates everywhere to ubiquitous language everywhere [DDD app.A p.313] `RULE`
